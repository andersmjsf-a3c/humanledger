# WS B2. Your writing-voice skill

A file that teaches the tool how you sound, built from things you actually wrote. When you finish this page you'll have a voice skill with your samples, your banned list, and the one rule that matters more than the list. About ninety minutes, most of it reading.

## Why this matters

The habit: don't ask it to write for you, ask it to draft, then correct it, and keep the corrections. What the corrections add up to is two things: a baseline every writer should demand, and then three rules that are only yours. This page builds both into one file.

Most people skip this worksheet because it feels like vanity. It isn't. Anything the tool writes that goes out under your name is your name on it. A draft that sounds like the average of the internet costs you with every reader who knows you, and most readers can tell faster than they can say why.

Two warnings. Your voice skill will be wrong for weeks; most people rebuild theirs more times than they count before a draft reliably sounds like them, and a new model release still knocks it loose. And the banned list is the easy part and the part that does the least. The work is in the three rules at the end of this page, and in what you add to the file after every draft that gets past you.

## An example

*An illustration, not a real person.* The first page of a solo consultant's voice skill, condensed. The real one runs several pages.

```
# Skill: My voice
This skill serves [the consultant]. Use it for anything I'll send or
publish as myself: client emails, proposals, the newsletter.

## Who I am, in one paragraph
Independent consultant to mid-sized logistics companies; eleven years
on my own after a decade running operations inside two of them. I write
for owners and operations heads who have heard every pitch.

## What I sound like
- Direct and fact-driven. The claim, then the number, then the reason.
- I show the reasoning from fact to conclusion so the reader can argue
  with a step.
- Not hyperbolic. If a sentence would embarrass me across a table, cut it.
- Short paragraphs of uneven length. Contractions.

## Three rules that are mine
1. One real detail from my own work in every piece. NEVER invent one;
   leave a bracket.
2. Plain words. Use, not utilize. Fix, not remediate. Run, not orchestrate.
3. Specific over significant. Every claim gets a number, a name, a date,
   or a scene, or it goes.

## Words and phrases I never use
- "I hope this finds you well." "Circle back." "Leverage."
- Pivotal, transformative, game-changer, a testament to.
- Exclamation points.

## Rules
- Draft, then I edit. Never tell me it's finished.
- A flagged sentence gets rewritten into something specific, or cut.
  Never swapped for a synonym.
- When unsure of my view, ask. Don't invent it.
```

Notice what isn't in it: adjectives about style. "Confident," "warm," "authoritative" tell the tool nothing. Every line in that file is a thing it can check a sentence against.

## Your turn

Work through these in order. The samples come first because everything else is built from them.

1. **Collect three to five samples.** Things you wrote yourself, sent, and were happy with. An email that landed. A note to your team. A post. Not your best writing; your normal writing, 100 to 300 words each. Paste them into a conversation.
   _____

2. **Ask what they have in common.** Paste this: "Read these as samples of how I write. Tell me what they have in common: sentence length, paragraph length, how I open, how I close, how formal I am, which words recur, which words never appear, whether I use contractions. Be specific and quote me." Then argue with the answer. Some of what it finds is you. Some is a habit you'd rather drop. Keep only what you'd defend. Write the keepers here, three to five lines.
   _____

3. **Run the baseline audit.** Wikipedia's editors keep a free page called "Signs of AI writing." Search for it and read it; twenty minutes. Then paste this: "Read Wikipedia's 'Signs of AI writing' guide. Go through my samples and flag every place one of its patterns appears: inflated significance, trailing participles, lists of three, 'experts say' with no expert, the summary paragraph, the hook question." Some flags will be yours. Write down the ones you recognize, because you've been letting drafts get away with them.
   _____

4. **Your banned list.** From step 3 and from every phrase the tool has written for you that made you wince. Ten to twenty items. Start with the ones that bother you most.
   _____

5. **Your three rules.** This is the page. read a sample from step 1 next to something the tool drafted for you this month, and name three things the first one has that the second one doesn't. A detail only you'd know. A word you'd use that it didn't. A place where it went grand and you'd have gone specific. Write each as a rule in one sentence, with an example of right and wrong.
   _____

6. **Who you are, in one paragraph.** Your role, what you know a lot about, how long you've done it, who you write for. Two or three sentences. Not a bio; the context a ghostwriter would need.
   _____

Now paste the frame below with your answers filled in, and ask the tool to tidy the wording without changing the meaning. Save what comes back as `my-voice.md`.

```
# Skill: My writing voice
This skill serves [your name]. Use it for anything I'll send or publish as myself.

## Who I am, in one paragraph
[answer 6]

## Three samples of how I write
[answer 1, pasted in full]

## What I sound like
[answer 2]

## Three rules that are mine
[answer 5, each with a right/wrong example]

## Words and phrases I never use
[answer 4]

## Rules
- Draft, then I edit. Never tell me it's finished.
- A flagged sentence gets rewritten into something specific (a number,
  a name, a date) or cut. Never swapped for a synonym.
- When unsure of my view, ask. Don't invent it.
```

**Test it once.** Open a fresh conversation, load the skill (or paste it at the top), and brief one real thing you need to send this week. Read the draft out loud, under your breath. Mark every sentence you wouldn't say. Those sentences are your first additions to the file.

**Check before it leaves.** Nothing leaves your hands unchecked, and a draft in your voice is the easiest thing to wave through because it sounds like you. Before you send anything this skill drafted, run the three-minute test: can you say what it says, out loud, without the screen? If you can't, it isn't yours yet.

*How to: the rewrite-don't-swap rule. The Wikipedia guide is explicit that replacing a banned word with a synonym hides the problem without fixing it. "Pivotal" becomes "key" and the empty claim behind it is untouched. When the tool flags a sentence, ask it what fact the sentence is standing in for. If there's a fact, the fact replaces the phrase. If there isn't, the sentence comes out.*

## Version 2, after a week of real use

Come back after you've drafted five or six real things with this skill and answer:

- Which sentences did you rewrite by hand more than once? Each one is a missing rule or a missing banned phrase.
- Did it reach for a detail about you that was wrong? Add "never invent; leave a bracket" if it isn't there, and add the right detail.
- Did it get something right that surprised you? Write down why, as a rule, so it keeps doing it.
- Read your three rules again. Would you still defend all three? Rewrite the one you wouldn't.

Make the changes. The file knows more about how you write than you did a week ago, and that gap keeps widening in your favor.

## Why this compounds

Every skill you build after this one that produces words, your brand skill in B3, your post skill in B6, the brief that becomes your business in C1, points to this file instead of describing your voice again. One correction here fixes every downstream draft at once. And the file gets read every time, so the drafts start closer to you than your own first drafts used to, and you spend your editing on the argument instead of the sound.
