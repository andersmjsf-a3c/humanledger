# WS B4. Your research-and-cite skill

A file that makes the tool show its trail every time you ask it to find something out. When you finish you'll have a research skill that names the decision, requires a source and a date on every fact, and ends with a line about what it couldn't verify. About forty-five minutes, including one real test.

## Why this matters

The research brief is the ordinary brief with two additions: the decision the research serves, and the sources you trust. Then four rules at the top of every request: every fact gets a source and a date, prefer primary sources, when two sources disagree show both, end with what you couldn't verify. If you've been typing those four lines every time, this page is where you stop.

Research is where the tool is most useful and most dangerous in the same breath. It reads faster than any analyst, and it will hand you a confident, well-organized answer built on a source that says something adjacent to the claim. The skill can't make it right. It can make it show you where every fact came from, so that you can be right.

Research you did yourself used to be slow, partial, and yours, and you trusted it because you'd done it. Research the tool does is fast, broad, and not yours until you've opened the sources. The skill is how you make it yours in ten minutes instead of a week.

## An example

*An illustration, not a real person.* A nonprofit director's research skill, condensed. Yours will have your own list of trusted sources.

```
# Skill: Research I can trust
This skill serves [the director]. Use it whenever I ask you to research,
look up, compare, find out, or "what's happening with."

## Before searching
- If I haven't said what decision this serves, ask. One question.
- If I haven't said which sources I trust for this topic, use the list
  below, and tell me which you used.

## Sources I trust, in order
1. The funder's own guidelines and published reports.
2. Government statistics and the original study.
3. The two national associations in our sector.
4. General press, only to find a primary source, never as the source.
5. Never: vendor blogs, anything selling a platform.

## While searching
- Search the web. Never answer a current-facts question from memory.
- Date every fact in the text: "as of September 2026."
- Older than six months on anything that moves: say so.
- Two sources disagree: show me both with their dates.
- Quote the sentence the fact comes from.

## Output
1. The answer, in one paragraph, written for the decision I named.
2. The facts, numbered, each with source, date, and the sentence.
3. The three sources worth my time and why.
4. A line that begins "What I couldn't verify:" followed by the list.
5. Links.

## Rules
- Never attach a claim to a source that didn't make it.
- No "experts say" or "studies show" without a name.
- If you can't find it, say so. "Couldn't find" is a finding.
- Don't round. Give the number the source gives.
```

The line that changes the most for most people is the "What I couldn't verify" line. Without it, research comes back looking complete, which is the most dangerous way research can look.

**Test it once.** Pick a real decision you're facing this month and run the related page's exercise through the skill: "I'm deciding whether to X, and I'll do it if Y." Then open the three sources it recommends and read the sentence behind the fact the decision turns on. Write down whether the sentence said what the tool said it did.

**Check before it leaves.** Research is the output people forward without reading, and nothing leaves your hands unchecked. Before any finding from this skill goes into a deck, an email, or a decision you'll have to defend, run the three-minute test: put away the materials and explain your argument, succinctly and convincingly, in three minutes or less, with the finding and where it came from. If you can't name the source without looking, you haven't read it yet.

*How to: turning on web search. Where the search toggle lives and whether it's on by default has changed more than once; see Part 0, worksheet 0.6, for the current steps. The rule in the skill ("turn on web search") works as a reminder to the tool, but it can't flip a setting you've turned off.*

## Version 2, after a week of real use

Come back after four or five real research requests and answer:

- Which source did it lean on that you wouldn't have? Move it down the list or add it to Never.
- Which source did it miss that you found yourself? Add it, with a line on when to use it.
- Did the "couldn't verify" line ever come back empty? It shouldn't. If it did, the skill is being agreeable; add "there is always at least one item here."
- Did you ever skip opening the sources because the answer looked complete? Write that down too. The skill can't fix that one. Only you can.

## Why this compounds

Every fact-bearing thing you build from here on runs through this file first: the fact-check pass in B5 is this skill's little sibling and reads the same source list, the posts in B6 cite from it, the deck in C5 is built on research it produced. The source list is the part that grows. After a quarter it's a map of your industry's reliable voices that no new hire could build in a month, and the tool reads it every time you ask a question.
