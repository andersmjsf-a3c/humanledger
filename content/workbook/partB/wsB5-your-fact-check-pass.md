# WS B5. Your fact-check pass

A short checklist the tool runs on anything with a number, a name, a date, or a quote in it, before you read it as a reader. When you finish you'll have the pass as a skill, and you'll have run it by hand once so you know what it's doing. About thirty minutes.

## Why this matters

One sentence belongs on the wall: nothing leaves your hands unchecked. Relying on the tool's output without checking it is the biggest mistake people make with AI. Not the only one. The biggest.

The pass has seven steps and takes five to ten minutes on a page by hand. This worksheet turns it into a skill so the tool runs it on every draft before you see it. Two warnings about that. The tool is checking its own work, so it will miss things, and it will miss them in the confident register that makes them hard to spot. And a pass that comes back clean gives you a page with fewer obvious errors in it, which is different from a page you can skip. You still read it, and the one number the argument turns on, you still check yourself.

Some managers now have a standing rule for their teams: before anything goes up the chain, the person who made it has to put away the materials and explain the argument, succinctly and convincingly, in three minutes or less. That's the test this site uses everywhere. The fact-check skill gets you to the point where you can pass it. It doesn't take the test for you.

## An example

*An illustration, not a real person.* The fact-check skill a two-person accounting practice might run on anything with a number in it, condensed. It is the shortest skill they own and the one they'd keep if they could only keep one.

```
# Skill: Fact-check pass
This skill serves [the partners]. Run it on any draft that contains a
number, a name, a date, a quote, or a claim about what a rule says,
before I read it. Run it again after I edit.

## Check every one of these
1. Numbers. Each has a named source and a date, in the text or in a
   note. Not "industry data." Flag any that don't.
2. The one that matters. Name the number the argument turns on. Find a
   second source for it. Say whether they agree.
3. Names and titles. Current and spelled right. Search for each one.
4. Dates. Correct, and consistent with each other and with the sources.
   For a rule: the final rule's date, not the proposal's.
5. Quotes. Found in the source they're attributed to, word for word.
   Under fifteen words. A quote you can't find is a paraphrase, attributed.
6. Arithmetic. Show the formula and the inputs. Add it up.
7. "Says" and "requires." The source actually says that, not something
   adjacent.

## Output
A table: item, status (confirmed / close but wrong / couldn't find),
source opened, what to change.
Then one line beginning "Couldn't verify:" for whatever's left.
Then the draft with only the flagged items changed, changes marked.

## Rules
- "Couldn't find" is a valid answer. Never upgrade it to "confirmed"
  to be helpful.
- Don't rewrite anything except the flagged items.
- Don't round, and don't pick the more impressive of two numbers.
- Any source you can't open is not a source.
```

The row that earns its keep is "close but wrong." A real source, a real number, and the number in the draft is from a different year, a different unit, or the sentence next to the one the tool read. Half of what people catch over a year is in that row.

1. **By hand, once.** Take something the tool wrote for you this week with at least three facts in it. For each fact: ask the tool for the single source and the sentence it comes from; open the source; read the sentence. Mark each one confirmed, close but wrong, or couldn't find. Write down how many of each you got.
   _____

2. **What kind of facts you handle.** Numbers in a budget. Names in a client memo. Dates in a contract. Quotes in a post. List the three or four kinds that show up in your work most, because the skill should check those first.
   _____

3. **Your "close but wrong" list.** What has the tool gotten almost right for you before? Wrong year, wrong title, wrong unit, a figure from a different market. Two or three lines. These go in as explicit checks.
   _____

4. **Who it's for.** What's the cost if a wrong fact gets through, in your work? A correction, a lost client, a legal problem. One sentence. The skill should know how careful to be.
   _____

5. **What you want back.** The table as above, or a shorter list? Do you want the draft changed or just flagged? Say so in one or two lines.
   _____

**Test it once.** Run the skill on the same draft you checked by hand in step 1. Compare its table to your marks. Where it caught something you missed, good. Where you caught something it missed, write down what kind of thing it was, because that's the kind you'll always have to check yourself.

**Check before it leaves.** The pass coming back clean is the start of the check, not the end. Before the draft goes anywhere, the three-minute test: put away the materials and explain your argument, succinctly and convincingly, in three minutes or less, naming where each of its three most important facts came from. If one of them is "the tool said it was confirmed," open the source yourself. Nothing leaves your hands unchecked, including the checker's work.

*How to: running two passes. The fact-check and the voice check (B2) are different passes; run both, in that order: facts first, because fixing a fact can change a sentence, then voice. If your setup lets you chain skills, chain them. If not, two messages: "run the fact-check pass," then "now run it against my voice."*

## Version 2, after a week of real use

Come back after the pass has run on five or six real drafts and answer:

- What got through that a reader caught? Add it as a numbered check, in the specific form it took.
- Did the "couldn't verify" line ever come back empty on a draft with more than five facts? Treat that as a failure of the skill, and add a line saying so.
- Which check never fires in your work? Leave it; it costs nothing. Which fires every time? Move it to the top.
- How many sources did you open yourself this week? If the answer is zero, the skill is working and you aren't.

## Why this compounds

This is the skill the others depend on. The research skill in B4 feeds it sources; the post skill in B6 won't release a draft until it has run; the deck in C5 logs every catch it makes next to yours and the standard's. And your "close but wrong" list, the one you started in question 3, becomes a record of exactly how the tool fails on your kind of facts, which is knowledge no one else has and the tool reads every time it checks your work.
