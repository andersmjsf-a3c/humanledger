# WS B6. Your post skill

A file for the posts you put up under your own name: open on a fact, hold one opinion, no tells, checked before it goes. When you finish you'll have a post skill that reads your voice file and your brand file instead of carrying its own copies, and you'll have run one real post through it. About forty-five minutes.

## Why this matters

What a post is: it opens on a specific fact, moment, or number, it holds one opinion you'd defend in the comments, and it ends on the point. Everything else about social media is format, and the format is where the tool fails, because it has read more posts than any human alive and the average of all of it is what it produces when you don't tell it otherwise.

Watch your own feed the day something happens in your field. Most of the posts restate the announcement with a sentence of enthusiasm on top and a row of hashtags underneath, and if you swapped the names on them nobody would notice. The post that does well is the one with an opinion in it and a file behind it that knows how its author states one.

This skill is short because it leans on two others. Your voice is in B2. Who your company is lives in B3. The post skill says what a post is and points at both. If you haven't built those two, build them first. A post skill with no voice behind it produces fluent posts by nobody.

## An example

*An illustration, not a real person.* A first-year founder's post skill, condensed. Note how little is in it. The voice and the brand live elsewhere.

```
# Skill: My posts
This skill serves [the founder]. Use it when I say "post," "LinkedIn,"
or "draft something on this." Load my voice skill and the brand skill first.

## What a post of mine is
- Opens on a specific fact, moment, or number from the news peg or from
  what we're building. Never on a question, a hook formula, or "I've
  been thinking."
- Holds exactly one opinion I'd defend in the comments. If the draft
  has two, ask me which.
- 120 to 200 words. Paragraphs of uneven length. Not one sentence per
  line down the screen.
- Ends on the point. Never on "Thoughts?" or "Agree?"
- Written for other founders and the operators we sell to.

## The brief I'll give you
Two or three sentences: what happened, what I think, who I'm talking to.
If I give you only the news, ask what I think before drafting.

## Never
- Hashtag walls. Emoji. Exclamation points. Rocket ships.
- "Excited to share." "Thrilled to announce." "Honored to." "Humbled."
- "Three things I learned when..." (the humble brag disguised as a lesson).
- Admiring the announcement. Admiration is a decision, not a default;
  push toward the harder read and let me pull back.
- A number that wasn't in the source.
- Anything about a customer or an investor by name.

## Before I see it
1. Run the fact-check pass on every number, name, and quote.
2. Check against the brand skill if it mentions the company.
3. Then show me the draft.

## Always
- Draft, then I edit, then I post it myself. You never post.
```

The last line is the one to keep if you cut everything else. The moment a post goes up is the moment it becomes yours in public, and your hand should be on it.

**Test it once.** Give it the brief from answer 6 and ask for the post. Then run the five passes yourself, one message each: is the opinion actually in it or did it drift into summary; cut it to your word range; fix every line you'd never say; check the facts; read it aloud and rewrite anything that sounds like a press release. Count how many of the five found something. Four or five is normal for a first draft.

**Check before it leaves.** A post is the fastest route from the tool to the public with your name attached, and nothing leaves your hands unchecked. Before you hit post, the three-minute test: put away the materials and explain your argument, succinctly and convincingly, in three minutes or less, saying what the post argues and where its fact came from, without the screen. If you'd be uncomfortable defending it in the comments tomorrow, it isn't finished. Then post it yourself.

*How to: the killed post. Sooner or later the fact pass will find a number in a draft that was never in the announcement, and you'll pull the post. The skill's "a number that wasn't in the source" line is the result. If you've killed a post for a reason, write that reason into Never, in its specific form. The specific form is the one the tool can check.*

## Version 2, after a week of real use

Come back after three or four real posts and answer:

- Which pass found something every time? That's a missing rule. Write it so the draft arrives with it already done.
- Did the draft ever open on a question or a hook? Make the opening rule more specific: name what it should open on, not just what it shouldn't.
- Did the opinion drift into summary? Add "state the opinion in the first fifty words" to What a post of mine is.
- Did it admire something you'd have questioned? Sharpen answer 5. The tool pushes toward the average take; the file is how you push back.

## Why this compounds

Every post now arrives already in your voice (B2), already checked against who your company is (B3), already through the fact pass (B5), and you spend the forty minutes on the argument instead of on fixing the sound. The "never" list grows with every post you almost regretted. And the brief habit in question 6, three sentences before you ask, is the same habit that writes the deck in C5 and the business brief in C1, practiced on something small every week.
