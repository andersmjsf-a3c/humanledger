# WS C5. Your 23 passes

One real deliverable, built with the tool against a standard you chose, with every pass logged and every catch credited. When you finish, you'll have the thing itself, plus a page that shows you where your judgment lives. About two hours, spread over a morning.

## Why this matters

A good deck takes twenty passes. It always did. What changed is that each pass now costs a minute instead of an afternoon, and every version that matters still starts with a note from you, and every version that goes out still has to be checked against a standard you chose. Diligence didn't get cheaper. It got faster.

This page has you do that once, on purpose, and keep score. Not because the score matters. Because the two columns you'll fill in at the end, catches that came from you and catches that came from the tool, are the clearest picture you'll ever get of what human-led AI means in your own work. You'll see what the machine is for. You'll see what only you can do. And you'll stop being surprised that it takes twenty passes, because you'll have watched twenty passes turn something competent into something you'd sign.

Pick something real. A deck you owe someone, a proposal, a one-pager, a report. Something with a reader who matters and a deadline that's close enough to feel.

One more reason to keep score. Your reader knows these tools exist. When the checks are this cheap, a wrong number or a claim you can't back no longer reads as a rough draft; it reads as a decision not to check. The passes aren't polish. They're the part your reader is quietly grading.

## An example

*An illustration, not a real person.* A first-year founder's morning, condensed to the parts you'll fill in yourself.

**The deliverable.** A pitch deck for a seed investor, cut from a 24-slide company overview. Eight slides maximum.

**The room.** One partner who has read every market-stats slide in the category this year. She needs to leave knowing what the company does, why now, and what the money buys. She'll stop trusting the founders at the first claim they can't back.

**The standard.** A widely cited published guide to seed decks: one piece on what the deck has to say, one on how it has to look.

**The log,** ten of the twenty-two rows:

| Pass | My note | What changed | Caught by |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Eight slides max | 24 cut to 8 | me |
| 2 | She knows the stats; open on the need | Stats out, need in | me |
| 4 | Score against the guide | Team slide moved from 7 to 2; traction slide had no numbers | standard |
| 6 | Revenue targets we won't hit by the date on the slide | Targets became adoption milestones | me |
| 8 | Customer named on slide 5 is under NDA | Name out, "a regional bank" in | tool |
| 9 | Product name spelled two ways | Fixed on both pages | tool |
| 11 | Speaker notes would travel in a forwarded file | Notes stripped | tool |
| 14 | "Revolutionary" on slide 3 | Replaced with the number | me |
| 17 | Text overflow on slide 6 in the rendered PDF | Shortened | tool |
| 22 | Cover line to one sentence | Done | me |

**The two columns at the end.** Catches from the founder: 11, mostly about the room and about claims. Catches from the tool: 8, mostly about consistency, leaks, and rendering. Catches from the standard: 3, all about order and emphasis. That split is typical: the person catches what's wrong for the reader; the machine catches what's wrong on the page.

## Version 2, next time you build one

The deliverable itself won't repeat; the way you built it will. After you've done this once:

- Which notes did you give that you'll give every time? (Those become rules in a skill for this kind of deliverable.)
- What did the standard catch that you should have caught? (Add it to the brief next time, so the first draft starts there.)
- What did the tool catch that you'd rather it always check? (Say so in the skill: "before every version, check that product names match across pages.")
- How many passes did it take? Write the number down. It'll be lower next time, and it'll never be one.

## Why this compounds

Every catch in your column is a piece of judgment you now know you own; every catch in the tool's column is a check you can hand off permanently by writing it into a skill. The log turns one morning's work into rules for every deliverable after it. And the pass count, written down, cures the thing that stops most people: the belief that if it took twenty tries, they did something wrong.
