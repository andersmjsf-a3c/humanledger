# Write something that sounds like me

## The rule

Don't ask it to write for you. Ask it to draft, then correct it, and keep every correction somewhere it will be read next time.

Here's why. The tool has read more email, more posts, and more reports than any person alive, and when you say "write this," it gives you the average of all of them. The average is fluent, tidy, and nobody's. It isn't yours, and the people who know you can tell in a sentence, usually before they can say why.

The fix is a different first move. Brief it like a colleague who's drafting for your signature: who this is for, what you want to happen, what you don't want to sound like, how you'd say it across a table. Then ask for a draft. Then read the draft and send it corrections one at a time: a sentence you'd never say, a word you'd never use, a place where it went grand and you'd have gone specific. Three or four rounds gets you a draft you'd sign.

The part most people skip is the last one. Those corrections are the one thing you produced today that you'll use again, and if they stay in the conversation they're gone tomorrow. Write them down. After a week you'll have a list, and that list is the beginning of a file the tool reads before it writes anything for you. That file is called a skill, and it's how you stop correcting the same thing twice.

## Try it now

Open your AI and paste this, with your details filled in:

> I need to send [what: an email, a note, a short update] to [who]. What I want to happen: [one sentence]. What I don't want to sound like: [two or three words: salesy, stiff, apologetic]. If I were saying this across a table I'd say: [one or two sentences, the way you'd actually talk]. Draft it. Keep it under [length]. Don't tell me it's finished; I'll edit it.

Read what comes back once. Then, one message at a time: tell it one sentence you'd never say and what you'd say instead; tell it one word to cut; ask for a version two-thirds the length. Read the last version out loud under your breath. Anything that sounds like a press release, send back.

## What it looks like

*An illustration, not a real person.* Say you're a solo consultant with six clients, and one of them has gone quiet for three weeks after a proposal. You ask the tool to "write a follow-up email" and get four paragraphs opening with "I hope this message finds you well" and closing with "I look forward to hearing from you at your earliest convenience." You've never written either sentence in your life.

So you start over with the brief above. Who: a client who went quiet; probably busy, possibly cooling. What you want: a yes, a no, or a date. What you don't want to sound like: needy. Across a table you'd say: "Haven't heard back, and I'd rather know where this stands than guess. Still interested, or should I close the file?" The draft comes back at three sentences. You cut one word and send it. The client replies that afternoon.

The three corrections you made (no greeting-card openers, no "at your earliest convenience," ask the plain question) go in a note. Next week you'll make them again, and the week after that you won't have to.

## Keep this

If the draft worked, the corrections are worth keeping. **Turn them into a file** opens the writing-voice builder (workbook B2), pre-filled with what you typed above. It asks for three things you've written yourself and liked, finds what they have in common, and builds the file the tool reads before every draft. Takes about an hour. The result is yours, stays yours, and gets better every time you catch it.
