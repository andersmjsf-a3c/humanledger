# Keep it from forgetting who I am

## The rule

Every new conversation starts with a stranger. If you want it to know you, you have to put that knowledge somewhere it will be read.

This is the single most common frustration, and it's a design fact, not a flaw. A conversation is a room. What you say in it stays in it. Open a new one and the tool has no idea you're a consultant who hates exclamation points and is in the middle of a proposal for a logistics client. It can't; nothing carried over.

Three places context can live, and each is right for different things. Memory is for who you are: a paragraph about you, your role, how you like work done, that the tool keeps across every conversation once you've told it. A project is a room with its own walls: one body of work, with its files and instructions, so your proposal work doesn't leak into your newsletter work and vice versa. A skill is for a job: the instructions for a thing you do repeatedly, loaded when you name the job.

Who you are goes in memory. What you're working on goes in a project. How you do a job goes in a skill. The common mistake is putting everything in the conversation and being surprised tomorrow.

## Try it now

Write the paragraph about yourself you'd want the tool to know every time. Who you are, what you do, what you're working toward this quarter, and three things about how you like work done. Keep it to what you'd be comfortable with a colleague reading. Then paste it with:

> Here's who I am and how I like to work. Remember this for future conversations: [your paragraph].

Whether that saves depends on your AI's memory setting being on, and where that setting lives has moved more than once; the Where things live page has today's answer. Then pick one body of work you're in the middle of, make a project for it, and put the two or three files you keep re-attaching inside. Tomorrow, open a new conversation in that project and notice what you didn't have to say.

## What it looks like

*An illustration, not a real person.* Say you're a solo consultant and you've noticed that every conversation starts with the same three minutes: I'm an independent consultant, I work with mid-sized logistics companies, I'm writing a proposal for one now, please don't use bullet points, please keep it under a page.

You split that into its three homes. The first two sentences and the two preferences go into memory as a paragraph. The proposal gets a project, and the client's brief, your rate sheet, and the last proposal you were proud of go in it as files. The proposal format itself (sections, length, tone) becomes a skill, because you write six of these a year.

The next morning you open a conversation inside the project and type "draft the proposal." The tool knows who you are, has the client's brief in front of it, and follows your format. The three minutes are gone. What's left is the part only you can do: deciding whether the proposal is right.
