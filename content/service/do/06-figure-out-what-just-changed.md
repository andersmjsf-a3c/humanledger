# Figure out what just changed

## The rule

The platform you're using is not finished and never will be. Plan for that instead of being surprised by it.

Three kinds of change hit you. A setting moves: the toggle you found last month is somewhere else, or has a new name. A name changes: a feature you read about in an article no longer appears under that word, because it was folded into something else. And the model changes: the company behind the tool releases a new version, and the thing you're talking to has slightly different habits than it did on Monday. Your drafts read a little differently. A rule it always followed, it now follows most of the time.

None of these are your fault, and none of them mean you were doing it wrong. The habits in these pages (brief it, correct it, check it, write the rules down) don't change when the buttons do. What changes is where the buttons are and how firmly the rules hold.

So two practices. Keep your rules somewhere that survives the room: a file, not a conversation. And after a release, re-test. Run your most-used skill on a task you've run before and compare the results. Where it drifted, add a line to the file. Where a setting moved, this site's Where things live page is the dated record, and it gets updated first.

## Try it now

Open your AI and ask it directly:

> Which model am I talking to right now, and when was it released? Has anything about how you work changed recently that I should know about: memory, projects, skills, search?

Then take one thing the tool has done for you reliably (a weekly summary, an email in your voice) and ask for it again today, the same way you did last time. Read the two side by side. If something slipped, you've found a line that belongs in a file rather than in your head.

Then look at the Where things live page on this site. If what you see on your screen doesn't match what the page says, tell us. That page is only useful if it's current, and you're how we know.

## What it looks like

*An illustration, not a real person.* Say you teach high school and run your department, and for a semester you've used the tool to turn your rough lesson notes into a parent-facing weekly update. It's been reliable: plain language, no jargon, under two hundred words, the week's topic in the first sentence.

One Monday the update comes back at three hundred words with a cheerful opening line you'd never write and a bulleted list where there used to be a paragraph. Nothing you did changed. You ask the tool which model you're talking to, and it names one you haven't heard of, released the previous week.

You re-brief the three rules that slipped (length, no bullets, topic first), and the next draft is fine. Then you do the thing that matters: you write those three rules into the file you keep for this job, dated, with a note that the new model loosened them. Next release, you'll re-test this job first, because now you know it's the one that drifts.
