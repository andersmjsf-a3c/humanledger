# Teach it a job I do every week

## The rule

If you've explained something to the tool three times, stop explaining and write it down.

Every conversation starts from nothing. The tool doesn't remember that your weekly report opens with the number, not the narrative, or that your client summaries never use the word "leverage," or that you want three options and a recommendation, never five. So you say it again. And again. Most people are re-briefing the same five things in every conversation and calling it "getting the hang of it."

A skill is the file you write instead. It says what the job is, who it's for, what you'll hand over each time, the steps, what the finished thing looks like, and the rules you'd never let anyone break. The tool reads it before it starts, so the first draft arrives already knowing what you'd otherwise spend ten minutes saying. You name the job; it does the job.

Two things to expect. The first version will be wrong in ways you can't see until you use it. And you'll be changing it as often as you use it for a while, which feels like failure and is the process. A skill is never finished. Every correction you'd otherwise make again goes into the file, and after a month the file knows your job better than a new hire would.

## Try it now

Pick the thing you do every week that has the same shape each time. Paste this:

> I'm going to describe a job I do repeatedly, the way I'd explain it to someone covering for me. Turn it into a one-page set of instructions with these sections: what the job is and who it's for; what I'll hand you each time; the steps in order; what the finished result looks like (length, format, what comes first); rules that must always be true; things that must never appear; and one or two things to ask me about before guessing. Here's the job: [describe it in a paragraph, including the mistakes you've seen made].

Read what comes back. Fix the lines that aren't quite you. Then open a brand new conversation, paste the whole thing at the top, hand it this week's inputs, and ask for the job by name. Compare the result to what you'd have made by hand. The differences are your first edits to the file.

## What it looks like

*An illustration, not a real person.* Say you manage a sales team of eight and every Monday you send your VP a pipeline summary. Every Monday you tell the tool the same things: open with the number against target, then what moved, then what's at risk, then what you need from her. Never more than a page. Never the word "robust." Always a date on every deal.

The third Monday you write it down instead. One page: the job, the inputs (the pipeline export and your notes), six steps, the format, four always-rules, three never-rules, and one question it should ask you (which deals you've already discussed with the VP so it doesn't repeat them). You save it and name it "Monday summary."

The fourth Monday you paste the export, type "Monday summary," and get a draft that's eighty percent right. You fix the other twenty percent and add two lines to the file. By the eighth Monday you're editing for judgment, not format. The twenty minutes you used to spend briefing are gone, and the summaries are more consistent than when you wrote them yourself.

## Keep this

What you just made is a skill, and it's worth keeping somewhere the tool can read it every time instead of pasting it. **Turn it into a file** opens the skill builder (workbook B1), pre-filled with what you wrote above, and saves it as a versioned file you can load into your AI. About an hour the first time, and the second skill takes half that, because the shape is the same.
