# Find out something I can rely on

## The rule

Research from the tool is only as good as the question, and the question has two parts most people leave out: what decision this serves, and which sources you trust.

Left alone, the tool answers the question you typed. "Tell me about the market for X" gets you a competent summary that reads like the first page of search results, because that's roughly what it is. It weighs sources by volume, and volume is not reliability. It will give you a confident number with no date on it, and the number may be three years old.

So the brief for research has everything a normal brief has (who you are, what you know, what good looks like) plus two lines. The decision: "I'm deciding whether to X, and I'll do it if Y." Now it knows what matters. The sources: whose filings, which agency, which publications you'd read yourself, and what it should never treat as a source. Now it knows where to look.

Then four rules at the top of every request: every fact gets a source and a date; prefer the original (the company's own statement, the study, the government page) over coverage of it; when two sources disagree, show both; end with a line that begins "What I couldn't verify." The last one is the line that makes the rest usable.

## Try it now

Pick a real decision you're facing this month. Paste this, filled in:

> I'm deciding whether to [X], and I'll do it if [Y]. What I already know: [two or three lines]. What would change my mind: [one line]. Sources I trust for this: [name two or three; say what you don't trust]. Search for current information. Give every fact a source and a date. Prefer original sources over articles about them. If two sources disagree, show me both. Give me the three sources most worth my time and why. End with a line that begins "What I couldn't verify:" and list anything I should treat as unconfirmed.

Open the three sources it recommends. Read the sentence behind the fact your decision turns on. Then decide.

## What it looks like

*An illustration, not a real person.* Say you direct a small nonprofit and your board wants to know whether to move the annual fundraiser online next year. You've asked the tool before and got a page of generalities about "the evolving landscape of virtual events."

This time: "I'm deciding whether to hold next year's gala online, and I'll do it if comparable organizations our size raised at least as much online as in person over the last two years. What I know: our in-person gala nets about a fixed amount after costs; a third of our donors are over seventy. What would change my mind: evidence that donor retention drops after a move online. Sources I trust: sector research from the national associations, published annual reports from organizations our size; not vendor blogs selling event platforms."

What comes back is narrower and more useful: three studies with dates, two of them agreeing and one not, a note that most of the data is from the pandemic years and may not hold, and a "couldn't verify" line saying it found no retention data for donors over seventy specifically. That last line is the one you bring to the board, because it's the question nobody had asked.
