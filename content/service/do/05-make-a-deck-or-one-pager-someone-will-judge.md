# Make a deck or a one-pager someone will judge

## The rule

Anything you hand to another person gets briefed for the room, checked against a standard that isn't yours, and read by you before it goes.

The tool can produce a deck in a minute, and the deck will be wrong in ways that cost you. Not wrong on the facts, usually. Wrong for the room: it explains things the reader already knows, it buries the point on slide six, it uses the word "revolutionary" where a number would do, and it carries something in the speaker notes that was never meant to leave the building.

So the brief starts with the reader. Who reads this, what they already know, what they must leave with, what would lose them. Then the draft. Then the step that changes everything: find a published standard for this kind of thing (a well-known essay on pitch decks, a style guide for memos, a checklist for board papers) and ask the tool to score your draft against it. Fix what fails. Then read it yourself, every page, as the reader would. Then the fact pass on every number. Then send.

Expect twenty passes, not two. The tool hasn't failed when that happens; twenty passes is what producing something good has always taken, and the only thing that changed is that each pass now costs a minute instead of an afternoon. Diligence didn't get cheaper. It got faster.

## Try it now

Pick one thing you have to hand to a specific person this week. Paste this:

> I'm making [a one-page summary / a short deck / a chart] for [who]. They already know: [two lines]. They must leave with: [one sentence]. What would lose them: [one line]. Here's what I have: [paste your notes or numbers]. Draft it. Then find a widely cited published guide on how to do this kind of document well, name it, and score the draft against it point by point. Tell me what fails and why.

Fix what fails. Then ask: "Is there anything in this that shouldn't leave the building: names, numbers, notes, claims we can't back?" Then read every page yourself, out loud if you can. Count the changes you made. That number is normal.

## What it looks like

*An illustration, not a real person.* Say you're a first-year founder and an investor asks for a short deck by Friday. Your first draft from the tool is twenty-two slides and opens with four slides on the size of the market. The investor has been looking at this market for five years. She knows.

You brief for the room: she knows the market; she needs to leave believing the two of you can build this and that someone will pay for it; a launch date you can't commit to would lose her. The redraft is nine slides. You ask for a score against a well-known seed-deck guide, and it flags that your "traction" slide has no numbers and your team slide is last when it should be second. You fix both. You ask what shouldn't leave the building, and it finds a customer's name on slide seven that's under NDA and a revenue figure in the speaker notes. Both come out.

Twenty-three versions by lunch, your own count. The investor's first question on Friday is about the team, which is the slide you moved.

## Keep this

The passes you just made are worth logging, because the pattern of what you catch, what the tool catches, and what the standard catches tells you what to put in a rule. **Turn it into a file** opens the deliverable log (workbook C5), which records one real piece of work pass by pass. If your deck is about your company, it also points you to the brand page (B3): who your company is, written down, so the tool can check every slide against it.
