# Check whether something it told me is true

## The rule

Nothing it gives you leaves your hands unchecked.

That's the one rule under everything else here, and the reason is simple. When the tool is wrong, it sounds exactly the way it sounds when it's right: confident, specific, well organized. It isn't lying. It fills gaps with what sounds plausible, and plausible is its specialty. A figure from the wrong year, a title from two jobs ago, a quote that's close to what someone said, a source that exists and says something adjacent. None of it looks wrong on the page.

So the check can't be "does this look right." The check is a short pass you run on anything with a number, a name, a date, or a quote in it, before it goes anywhere. Ask for the source of each fact and the sentence it comes from. Open the source. Read the sentence. Mark each fact confirmed, close but wrong, or couldn't find. Then write one line at the bottom listing what you couldn't verify, because that line is what makes the rest trustworthy to whoever reads it.

And the test for whether you're done: can you explain the thing out loud, for three minutes, without looking at the screen? If you can't, you haven't finished the work yet, no matter how finished the page looks.

## Try it now

Take something the tool wrote for you this week that has at least three facts in it. Paste this under it:

> For every number, name, date, and quote above: give me the single source it came from and quote the exact sentence from that source. If you can't find a source you can link to, say "couldn't find." Don't round, and don't pick the more impressive of two numbers. Then list anything you're not sure of.

Open every link. Read the sentence it quoted and compare it to the claim. Count how many came back confirmed, how many were close but wrong, and how many it couldn't find. The second category is the one to remember; it's where most of the damage lives.

If your AI has a web search setting, make sure it's on before you ask. Answering from memory is how it gets dates wrong.

## What it looks like

*An illustration, not a real person.* Say you run a two-person accounting practice and you've asked the tool for a client memo on a tax rule that changed this year. The memo is clean: a threshold, an effective date, a citation to the agency's own page. It reads like something you'd send.

You run the pass. The threshold is right. The agency page is real. The effective date is from the proposal, not the final rule, and the final rule moved it by six months. The citation is to a page that exists and discusses the rule but never states the date the memo gives. Two facts confirmed, one close but wrong, one source that didn't say what the memo said it did.

Ten minutes. The memo that goes out has the right date and a line at the bottom: "Threshold and rule confirmed against the agency's published guidance; effective date confirmed against the final rule, not the proposal." Your client never knows there was a first draft. That's the point.
